# Style guide

Principles adapted from J. Schimel, *Writing Science* (OUP 2012), and an academic writing workshop checklist. Paraphrased; see README for sources.

## Words
- Prefer well-known words. "Well-known" is measured by Lea's own writing (see `voice-samples.md`): field terms and common academic words are fine; avoid rare, ornate or inflated words. Where a simpler word says the same, use it:
  use (not utilise), show (not demonstrate), start (not initiate), let (not permit), need (not necessary), try (not attempt), method (not methodology), length/time (not duration).
- Use strong action verbs. Replace fuzzy verbs (occur, affect, facilitate, perform, conduct, implement) with verbs that say *what* happened (increase, decrease, inhibit, disrupt, accelerate, modify).
- Un-bury verbs (avoid nominalisations): "A influenced B" not "A had an influence on B"; "we analysed" not "we performed an analysis of". Watch words ending in -tion, -ment, -ity.
- Cut empty amplifiers: very, quite, rather, really, highly, entirely, certainly, substantially, dramatically, simply.
- Cut rhetorical and marketing words: remarkably, interestingly, surprisingly, groundbreaking, revolutionary, unprecedented, first ever. Novelty claims must be tied to a specific limitation.
- Declarative, not intentional: "we investigated / tested / implemented / defined / consider", never "we try to / aim to / attempt to / explore whether".
- Cut empty phrases: "in order to" -> "to"; "it is possible that" -> "may"; "there is a chance that" -> "may"; "due to the fact that" -> "because".
- Avoid jargon; define every abbreviation at first use.
- Compound nouns may replace prepositional phrases ("the childhood obesity epidemic" not "the epidemic of childhood obesity"), but avoid noun trains of 4+ nouns.
- British spelling: analyse, modelling, behaviour, colour, organisation, centre, labelled.

## Pronouns and referents
- Never start a sentence or clause with a naked "it", "this", "these", "that", "they". Name the referent: "This approach...", "These differences...", "The model...".
- If the referent is unclear, repeat the noun.

## Sentences
- Short, clear topic at the start; main verb immediately after the subject.
- Familiar/old information first, new/important information last (stress position).
- Keep subject and verb close; do not insert long clauses between them.
- One idea per sentence. Long sentences need a lead-then-development structure.
- Prefer active voice when the actor matters ("We measured..."). Passive is fine in methods when the actor is obvious.
- Parallel structure in lists and comparisons.

## Linking (flow)
- Link sentences by repeating the key word: the stress (end) of one sentence becomes the topic (start) of the next.
- Keep the key term identical throughout a text. Do not use synonyms for the same concept.
- Lea's example of the pattern:
  "We looked at five scenarios implementing different versions of X. The implementations were all restricted to only allow Y. Despite these restrictions, we noticed..."
- Word repetition is the main link between sentences. Use a transition word only where it signals real logic (contrast, consequence), not as a mere connector. The explicit ABT transitions ("However", "Yet", "Therefore") belong mainly in the abstract and at key turns of the story.
- Write a story, not a list: show how points relate rather than leaving the reader to infer it.

## Condensing
- Brevity comes from selecting, not compressing. First prune whole points, then shorten sentences.
- Delete: redundancies, the obvious, unnecessary modifiers, metadiscourse ("In this section we will describe..."), verbosity.
- Declutter search list: "of", "that/which", "this/that/these/those", forms of "to be", -tion/-ment words, "and/as well as/too/or".
- Every word must add content, clarify meaning or provide coherence.

## Hedging, tense, limitations
- Hedge appropriately: not more than the data require, not less. State the main result without a hedge (avoid "Our results suggest..." for the main result in the abstract); hedge only the interpretation. Resolutions and take-home messages are not the place for uncertainty.
- Methods and results: past tense. Established knowledge and figure descriptions: present tense.
- Figures support the text; they do not carry the logic. Describe the finding in words; avoid bare "As shown in Fig. 2" and caption retelling.
- Methods limitations belong in the methods; limitations of interpretation belong in the discussion. State limitations openly and early rather than hiding them.
