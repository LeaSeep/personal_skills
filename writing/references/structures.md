# Structures

Always make the structure explicit. In drafting, propose one of these and let Lea confirm.

## ABT: And, But, Therefore (default for abstracts and short texts)
1. **And** - context: what is known and why it matters.
2. **But** - the gap or problem, introduced with "However", "Yet", "But".
3. **Therefore** - what we did, implemented or investigated. Default wording: "Therefore, we..." / "We therefore...". Alternative: one "Here, we..." sentence that names the method and the concrete result (group checklist style).
4. Results - what we found.
5. **So what** - what the findings mean and enable.

## Story structures (Schimel)
- **OCAR** - Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution. Slow build; classic paper structure.
- **LDR** - Lead, Development, Resolution. Point first; fast. Use for discussions and high-impact journals.
- **LD** - Lead, Development. Fastest; everything up front. Use for results paragraphs and abstracts.
- **ABDCE** - Action, Background, Development, Climax, Ending. Front-loaded; use for proposals.

## Paragraph structures
- **Point first (default, ~75% of paragraphs):** topic sentence with the message, then development sentences.
- **Point last (~25%):** at key transitions in the story; build up and end on the message. Needs a strong opening and ending.
- **Lea's standard opening pattern (Results and Methods paragraphs):** one or two sentences of context -> "We therefore investigated / tested / checked / implemented..." -> the rest (what we found, what it means). In the Introduction, "we" appears only in the final contribution paragraph.
- Each paragraph: one single, clear point. Each new topic gets a resolution.

## Paper sections
- **Introduction (funnel):** P1 broad field context -> P2 specific subfield -> P3-4 synthesised literature (trends, not paper-by-paper) -> limitation paragraph (concrete gap derived from the literature, and why it matters) -> final contribution paragraph ("Here, we..." / "To learn X, we did Y", method named, main result). No gap statement before the synthesis. An introduction identifies the boundary of knowledge; it is not a literature review.
- **Methods:** each subsection opens with a declarative scope statement ("We define...", "We consider..."); define every variable before use; keep conceptual model, statistical model, algorithm and evaluation in separate paragraphs. No motivation in Methods.
- **Results:** each subsection = setup/question (context -> "We therefore tested...") -> findings described in text with quantitative detail -> short mini-conclusion linking to the next subsection. Subsection headings state the conclusion ("Method X reduces bias on dataset A", not "Evaluation on dataset A"). No speculation, no interpretation beyond the data. Statistics support the story; they are not the story.
- **Discussion:** LDR. Opening paragraph restates the problem and synthesises the key findings (no figure references, no detailed numbers). Middle paragraphs: every paragraph answers "So what?" in its topic sentence, relates the findings to the literature from the introduction, and states scope (where it generalises, where not). Limitations stated openly, without defensive tone. Final paragraph: broader implication, concrete future direction; never "more research is needed", no new data. Build to the resolution.
- **Conclusion / resolution:** (1) summarise key results, (2) synthesise them, (3) state how they advance understanding of the opening problem. Match the scope of the opening: do not over-promise or under-deliver.

## Self-check per section
1. Is there a single, clear point?
2. Are the links between paragraphs clear?
3. Do any extra words break the flow?
4. Does each new topic have a resolution?
5. Is every unit introduced by a heading or a clear opening sentence?
